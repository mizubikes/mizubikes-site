Most of us don't ride pure gravel. A typical ride starts on pavement, links into a dirt road or a stretch of singletrack, then comes home on pavement again. That kind of ride rewards a tire that rolls fast on asphalt and still holds its line when the surface turns loose. It doesn't reward a knobby tire built for mud or a slick built for a paved bike path.

I went through this year's group tests and buyer's guides to see which tires actually do that. I haven't ridden any of these myself, so this is a read of the data and the reviewers' conclusions. I've also tried to flag where the tests disagree.

## What "mixed" should mean for your tire choice

The best-reviewed mixed-surface tires share a recipe: a low, closely spaced tread, a supple casing and a fast rubber compound. A German test of ten tires on road and gravel came to that conclusion. It ended with the Continental Terra Speed as overall winner and described the best mixed-terrain tires as low-profile with quick compounds. Tires like that get close to road-tire feel on pavement and still get through rough sections at a sensible pace.

The tradeoff is flat protection and mud. Fast, thin casings are the ones that pinch-flat and puncture more easily, and a low tread clogs and slides when dirt turns wet. If your "dirt" is chunky rock or your region gets a rainy season, the answer shifts.

## The fast all-rounders

The [Continental Terra Speed](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Continental+Terra+Speed+gravel+tire&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the benchmark here. In Granfondo's 2026 test, the 45mm version weighed 530 g and measured 12.5 W of rolling resistance. The group average was about 15 W. Its fine tread also gripped better than you'd expect, especially on dry, dusty surfaces. The catch is pinch-flat protection, where it scored just 1 out of 10 in that test. German pricing in that test was €57.90, and if you run it tubeless at sensible pressures that's a manageable tradeoff. If your local trails are rocky, it isn't.

The [Schwalbe G-One RS Pro](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+RS+Pro&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the speed pick. It had the lowest rolling resistance in Granfondo's 17-tire race test at 10.6 W, and Velo picked it as its best overall gravel tire for 2026. The tester there liked that it was both fast and tacky. It listed at €74.90 in the same German test. The Terra Speed beat it in mud, though, so it's a fair-weather choice.

The [Hutchinson Caracal Race](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Hutchinson+Caracal+Race&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the third fast option. Velo reports that it sits at or near the top of two independent rolling-resistance leaderboards across its 40 to 50mm sizes. Cycling Weekly also ranks it second overall as its best fast gravel tire. If your mix leans toward pavement and smooth dirt, it deserves a look.

There's also the Pirelli Cinturato Gravel line. BikeRadar named the [Cinturato Gravel RC](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Pirelli+Cinturato+Gravel+RC&tag=mizubikes-web-20) its best mixed tire for its grip, durability and dirt-shedding tread. A German review from this year says Pirelli has since dropped the RC from its lineup, so check stock before you plan around it. The harder-compound [Cinturato Gravel H](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Pirelli+Cinturato+Gravel+H&tag=mizubikes-web-20) was in Granfondo's race test at €79.90 in 45mm.

## When you want more bite

If your dirt is looser, or you carry more weight, step up a notch in tread.

The [Maxxis Rambler](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Maxxis+Rambler+gravel+tire&tag=mizubikes-web-20) was described by Granfondo as the comfort king of Maxxis's second-generation gravel line. Its 45mm version weighed 557 g and measured 14.8 W, with strong flat protection from the EXO casing, at €59.90. That's a few watts behind the fastest tires, but the grip and casing may be worth it if you ride rough roads.

The [Schwalbe G-One RX Pro](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+RX+Pro&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is the grippy one. Granfondo called it the go-to for wet, technical or muddy races, since no other tire in the test combined that much grip with such low rolling resistance. It's more tire than most mixed-pavement rides need, but it makes sense in a wet climate.

The [Schwalbe G-One Overland](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+Overland&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is Cycling Weekly's best overall. Cycling Weekly describes it as built for riders who combine asphalt, gravel and a bit of dirt, with a good balance of grip, speed and toughness. A German long-term test agreed that it's tough but found it noticeably slower on asphalt than the Terra Speed. That test's 50mm sample weighed 625 g. The version tested has since been discontinued, and the current Performance RaceGuard version is listed at 590 g.

Among the cheaper, more widely stocked options is the [Specialized Pathfinder Pro](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Specialized+Pathfinder+Pro+2Bliss+Ready&tag=mizubikes-web-20). Specialized lists the 700x42 at about 540 g, and sizes run from 32mm to 47mm in 700c. Prices vary a lot by retailer, so shop around.

Finally, there's one new option. Panaracer's [GravelKing ZX](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Panaracer+GravelKing+ZX&tag=mizubikes-web-20) is a semi-slick with a three-zone tread, pitched at courses where the surface keeps changing. According to Panaracer's rollout schedule, it comes in 35 to 55mm widths and starts at $64.99 per tire. I haven't seen independent rolling-resistance numbers for it yet, so I'd wait for those before buying on hype.

## A quick word on those watts

The rolling-resistance figures I've quoted come from Granfondo's drum tests. They're useful for ranking tires in one test, but not for comparing across tests. Different labs use different pressures, speeds and drums.

The same test makes a good point about weight: a lighter tire isn't automatically a faster one. Vittoria's tires weigh roughly 700 g yet land in the middle of the field for rolling resistance. Use the numbers to narrow your list, not to pick a winner.

Flat protection is the other number to look at. Challenge's Getaway XP scored a perfect 10 for pinch-flat resistance in Granfondo's test, at 665 g and 15.2 W. That's a real cost in speed, but if you ride rocky terrain, it may be a worthwhile trade.

## Fit, pressure and setup

Before buying, check your frame clearance with the actual tire and rim, not just the number printed on the tire. I covered that in [Gravel Bike Tire Clearance: What the Specs Don't Tell You](/posts/gravel-bike-tire-clearance/). Measured widths often differ from claimed ones, and some tires run narrow or tall.

If you're going tubeless, have a plan for flats on the road. [5 Road Bike Saddle Bag Essentials For Tubeless Road Bikes](/posts/5-road-bike-saddle-bag-essentials-for-tubeless-road-bikes/) covers the basic kit. For pressure, one German test suggested 1.6 to 2.5 bar (about 23 to 36 psi) for 45mm tires. Your weight, rim width and surface all move that number, so treat it as a starting point.

If you're curious how far the speed obsession goes, I wrote about it in [Gravel Bikes Keep Getting Faster. I'm Not Sure Anyone Asked For That.](/posts/gravel-bikes-do-we-need-more-speed/). Tires are where it's most useful, since a fast tire rewards you on every ride.

## What I'd buy

For a mix of pavement and hardpack dirt in a dry climate like Southern California, I'd start with the Terra Speed or the G-One RS Pro. Both have the lab results and the reviewer praise to back them up. I'd pick the Terra Speed if I wanted the better price and a bit more mud tolerance. I'd go with the RS Pro if I wanted the lowest rolling resistance.

If my dirt were rougher, I'd move to the Rambler or Overland and accept the extra watts. A flat 30 miles from home costs more than a few watts. Tire choice is a tradeoff, and the right answer depends on your local roads.
