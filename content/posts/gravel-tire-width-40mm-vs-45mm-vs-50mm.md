**Quick answer:** For gravel tire width 40mm vs 45mm vs 50mm, 45mm is the best default for most riders. Pick 40mm if your rides are mostly pavement and hardpack, and 50mm if the terrain is rough, loose or loaded with bikepacking gear. The gaps between these sizes are smaller than the gaps between a good tire and a bad one.

## Gravel tire width 40mm vs 45mm vs 50mm at a glance

The table below pairs each width with what it suits. The weights are claimed figures for one tire family, the Schwalbe G-One RS Pro, so you can see how much each step costs. Other tires will differ.

| Width | Best for | Claimed weight (G-One RS Pro) | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40mm | Pavement links, hardpack, fast group rides | 445 g | Less cushion on washboard and loose rock |
| 45mm | Mixed gravel, the do-it-all choice | 515 g | Slightly heavier, needs a frame that clears it comfortably |
| 50mm | Rough roads, singletrack bits, bikepacking | 585 g | More weight, more drag on smooth surfaces |

Those weights come from a US retailer listing, and BikeRadar's scale put its 50mm sample at 581 g. Expect roughly 70 g more per tire for each 5mm step in this family. Pairs add up to 140 g or so between 40mm and 50mm, which matters less on a gravel bike than on a road bike.

## Is wider actually faster?

This is where the internet gets tidy and the data doesn't. Cyclingnews ran an earlier gravel tire lab test that found wider tires lost fewer watts to rolling resistance. Its later combined aero and rolling resistance work on gravel tires, from 38mm up to 55mm, found both aero drag and rolling resistance rose as the tires got bigger.

The likely reason is that bigger gravel tires often come with beefier casings and thicker tread. Take Schwalbe's G-One RS Pro. BicycleRollingResistance.com measured 15.7 W for the 40mm and 18.2 W for the 50mm. It attributed the roughly 15% jump to a tread that grew from 2.0mm to 2.6mm thick, not to the width itself. The Specialized Pathfinder TLR went the other way. The 50mm tested about 15% lower in rolling resistance than the 45mm, though its wet grip score was lower.

So the lab numbers don't show a clean winner. Treat width as one variable and tread, casing and rim match as the others. Ridley, for example, claims a 38mm tire saves about 6 watts of aero drag over a 55mm. It also admits that rougher ground shifts the advantage back toward the bigger tire.

Pressure is the quiet factor. Cyclingnews found that sweeping a 45mm tire from 30 to 50 PSI changed rolling resistance by only about 8 watts. That means pressure you can't fine-tune because the tire is too narrow for the terrain costs you more than it saves. ## When 40mm is the right call

Forty millimeters still makes sense if your gravel is mostly smooth roads, rail trails and hardpack with long pavement stretches between. One width guide groups 30-40mm as the fast pavement and hardpack range, and notes it gets less forgiving on washboard and deep gravel.

It's also the safer choice on a frame with limited clearance, or on a race bike where aero matters. And it's the width where a few popular aero gravel wheels were designed to work best. Zipp's 303 XPLR SW rim is optimized for a 40mm tire, with Zipp saying losses are minimal at 45mm.

I'd expect the downside to show up on chunky roads. Narrower tires need more pressure to avoid pinch flats, and that costs comfort and grip.

## Why 45mm is the default

Granfondo-Cycling's 32-tire group test focused on 45mm because it considers that size the new sweet spot between efficiency, grip and comfort, and because it fits almost every modern gravel bike. That lines up with how bikes are built now. Many new frames clear 50mm or more, so a 45mm tire leaves useful space for mud and debris.

A 45mm tire is also the width where tire choice has the most room to shine. In that same test, the Schwalbe G-One RS Pro measured 10.6 W, the lowest in the field. Some other 45mm tires tested well above that. For a race-oriented pick in this width, look at the [Schwalbe G-One RS Pro 45mm](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+RS+Pro+700x45&tag=mizubikes-web-20), which one US retailer lists around $91.

## When 50mm earns its weight

Go to 50mm when the surface decides your day. One writer notes that 45-50mm tires handle roots, rocks and loose dirt more confidently on routes with singletrack, thanks to the extra air volume. Bikepacking loads add another argument, because a heavier bike benefits from more volume at a given pressure.

Be choosy about the tread, though. BicycleRollingResistance.com called the Schwalbe G-One RX Pro 50 a true off-road tire and the least suitable for asphalt among the 50mm tires it has tested. If your route is half pavement, a semi-slick 50mm like the [Schwalbe G-One RS Pro 50mm](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Schwalbe+G-One+RS+Pro+700x50&tag=mizubikes-web-20) fits better than a knobby one.

Don't fill your frame's clearance just because you can. One guide points out that a bike rated for 50mm tires can run 40-45mm with a healthy gap for mud. Check my posts on [real-world vs spec tire clearance](/posts/gravel-bike-tire-clearance/) before buying, since mounted width depends on the rim.

## Labels aren't measurements

A tire's stated width and its mounted width can differ. In Granfondo's test, a Michelin Power Gravel labeled 40mm measured 42.2mm, while a Continental Terra Speed labeled 45mm measured 43.8mm. Cyclingnews saw the same thing in a wheel test, where tires labeled 55mm came out under 55mm on every rim.

That means a true 43mm tire can be wider than a 40mm in the real world. Compare measured widths where you can find them, and leave margin for the frame.

## Which one to pick

Choose 40mm if you ride mostly pavement and hardpack, or your frame clears 42mm at best. Choose 45mm if you want one tire for a typical mix of dirt roads, pavement and the occasional rough section. Choose 50mm if you ride rough terrain, carry bags, or prioritize comfort over the last few watts. If you're stuck between two, go with the wider one and spend your effort on pressure and tread.

## FAQ

### Is 45mm the best gravel tire width?

For most riders, it's the best starting point. Granfondo-Cycling calls it the sweet spot between efficiency, grip and comfort, and it fits most modern gravel bikes. Riders on mostly smooth surfaces can still do well on 40mm.

### Are wider gravel tires faster?

Not always. Cyclingnews's later gravel testing, from 38mm to 55mm, found rolling resistance and aero drag both rose with width. Its earlier testing found wider tires were faster on rolling resistance alone, so tread and casing matter as much as size.

### Should I run 50mm tires if my frame clears them?

Only if your riding calls for it. A frame that clears 50mm lets you run a 40-45mm tire with plenty of mud space, so you don't have to fill the gap.
