The SRAM versus Shimano gravel question looks like a speeds argument: 13 versus 12. It mostly isn't. The real differences are what your frame accepts, how much you want to spend, and whether you want 1x, 2x, or cables.

Here is how the two systems compare as of November 2026, using published specs and US pricing. I haven't ridden these back to back, so the ride-feel points come from other reviewers' reporting.

## What you're actually choosing between

SRAM's gravel line is the XPLR family. Red, Force and Rival are all 1x13, wireless AXS, with a 10-46T cassette. The range works out to about 460%, per BikeRadar's [gravel groupset table](https://www.bikeradar.com/advice/buyers-guides/gravel-bike-groupsets). The derailleurs bolt straight to the frame, so they need a UDH dropout.

Shimano's GRX is a broader menu. You can get mechanical 12-speed in 1x or 2x, 2x12 Di2 (RX825), and 1x12 wireless Di2 (RX827, plus the cheaper RX717 parts). The 1x cassettes go to 10-51T, which BikeRadar lists at about 510% for the Di2 versions. The mechanical "Unbeatable" 10-45T setup is closer to 450%.

So the 13th cog gets you tighter steps in a narrower range, and Shimano gets you more range or a second chainring. That is the actual tradeoff.

## The frame requirement is the first filter

This is the part people skip. The Rival XPLR rear derailleur requires a frame with a UDH-compatible interface, and the Force version is the same. If your frame has a conventional hanger, SRAM's top three gravel groups won't fit. I covered this in [Check the Dropout Before You Commit to SRAM 1x13](/posts/udh-sram-1x13-gravel-frame-compatibility/), and the [UDH-compatible gravel bikes list](/posts/udh-compatible-gravel-bikes-2026/) shows which frames are covered.

Shimano's GRX has no such restriction. It mounts the conventional way, so it suits older frames, used bikes, and anything you don't want to replace. The freehub differs too. SRAM needs XDR. GRX 1x12 uses Micro Spline, while the 2x12 Di2 cassettes work with an HG freehub.

If your frame isn't UDH, that rules out SRAM's 13-speed XPLR groups and the choice is mostly made for you.

## Gearing: tight steps versus wide range

SRAM's 10-46T cassette runs 10-11-12-13-15-17-19-21-24-28-32-38-46. That is a lot of single-tooth steps at the fast end, which suits fast group rides and gravel racing on rolling terrain. I went through the numbers in [SRAM Force E1 2x vs 1x vs XPLR: Gearing and Gear Jumps Compared](/posts/sram-force-e1-gearing-gear-jumps/).

The catch is the top of the range. A 46T low gear with a typical 40-44T ring is fine for most gravel. It is less friendly if you ride loaded bikepacking rigs up steep dirt. Shimano's 10-51T gives you a noticeably easier bailout gear.

Shimano's 2x option answers the same problem differently. GRX 2x12 pairs a 48/31 crankset with an 11-34 or 11-36 cassette. That gives tight steps and a low gear at the same time. The cost is a front derailleur and a bit more fiddling. SRAM's XPLR line is 1x only. You can get SRAM 2x by running road Force or Rival AXS with the wide 43/30 crankset, but then you're buying a road groupset with road gearing, not a gravel-specific one. If you want a 2x drivetrain built for gravel, GRX is the one.

Shimano also fixed two long-standing gaps in April 2026. The RX820-level cranks now come in 160 and 165 mm lengths, and new 44T and 46T chainrings join the existing 40T and 42T. Smaller riders and faster racers had been asking for both.

## Price: what you'd actually pay in the US

SRAM Rival XPLR is the realistic entry point. [Performance Bicycle lists the Rival XPLR AXS groupset at $1,430](https://www.performancebike.com/sram-rival-axs-xplr-gravel-groupset-black-1-x-13-speed-10-46t-sramrival-e1grp2-bdl/p1644912). That bundle covers shifters with calipers, derailleur, cassette, chain, rotors, battery and charger, but not a crank. Launch pricing with the power meter crank was $1,743 for the complete Rival XPLR AXS groupset. Force XPLR was quoted at $2,358 with a power meter and $2,153 with standard cranks.

Parts prices matter if you're building up or replacing wear items. At US retail the [Force XPLR cassette is $305](https://www.rei.com/product/C04533/sram-force-xplr-xg-1371-cassette-13-speed) and the Rival cassette is $215. The Rival XPLR rear derailleur is $360 and the Force is $475. Those are the numbers to remember, because 13-speed cassettes cost real money. If you wear one out in a season of gritty riding, price a replacement before you commit. You can look up the [Force XPLR cassette](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=SRAM+Force+XPLR+XG-1371+cassette&tag=mizubikes-web-20) or the [Rival XPLR rear derailleur](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=SRAM+Rival+XPLR+AXS+rear+derailleur&tag=mizubikes-web-20) to compare current prices.

Shimano covers a much wider spread:

- **Mechanical GRX:** at the 2023 launch, the RX610 groupsets were $1,165.92 for 1x and $1,233.91 for 2x. Those are launch prices, so check what shops charge now. RX820 1x groupsets launched around $1,543 to $1,573.
- **Wireless Di2 1x12 (RX827):** complete groupset cost is US$2,282.
- **Budget wireless (RX717):** a parts-based route. US MSRP is $435 for the derailleur and $230 for the right shifter. BikeRadar describes it as lined up against SRAM Rival XPLR.

In short, Shimano is cheaper if you stay mechanical, and roughly even with Force XPLR if you go wireless 1x. Velo's early GRX Di2 review called it on par price-wise and slightly heavier than Force XPLR without a power meter.

## Weight

SRAM claims 2,686g for a complete Force XPLR AXS groupset and 2,961g for Rival, though the Rival figure includes a power meter. BikeRadar lists the Rival claim at 2,923g without one. Shimano's claimed weight for a complete RX827 groupset is 2,987.5g.

That is a few hundred grams at Force level and basically nothing at Rival level. Some of the gap is the derailleur: Shimano's RX827 is 449 grams against SRAM Rival's 435. I wouldn't pick a groupset on weight unless you're building a race bike and counting grams elsewhere too.

## Feel and ergonomics

Reviewers rate both highly, in different ways. Flow Mountain Bike's GRX Di2 review praised Shimano's lever shape and crisp shifting, and said that the hoods give more leverage and control than SRAM's Force AXS levers. That review covered the 2x system and an earlier SRAM lever generation. The current SRAM levers claim big gains in braking effort. Pedal Passion's roundup notes that professional testing has praised SRAM's intuitive controls and low-effort braking, while seeing Shimano as more adjustable.

Treat these as tendencies. Both brands brake well enough that lever reach and hand size will matter more than the badge. If you can try both on a shop bike, do it.

## Will Shimano go 13-speed?

There's chatter about it. One Italian site is speculating about a 13-speed GRX, but it says Shimano hasn't released official dates or confirmation. That's a rumor, so I wouldn't hold off a purchase for it. If you're buying now, the question is which of today's systems fits your bike and budget. If you can wait and ride your current setup, waiting costs nothing.

## Which one I'd buy

**Pick SRAM Rival or Force XPLR** if your frame is UDH, you want 1x, and you like the idea of tight jumps and wireless shifting at every tier. Rival gives you almost all of the system for the least money. It suits fast gravel, mixed-surface rides and rolling terrain. Budget for the cassettes.

**Pick GRX mechanical** if you want the lowest cost, simple cables, or a frame without UDH. A 1x12 with a 10-51T cassette is a perfectly capable setup, and the parts are widely compatible.

**Pick GRX Di2 2x12** if you ride a lot of pavement or group rides with your gravel bike, want tight steps and a low gear together, and don't mind a front derailleur.

**Pick GRX RX827 or RX717** if you want wireless 1x with a big climbing gear on a non-UDH frame. A [Shimano RX717 rear derailleur](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Shimano+GRX+RD-RX717+Di2+rear+derailleur&tag=mizubikes-web-20) with a 10-51T cassette is the cheapest way in. Remember that the 1x cassette needs a Micro Spline freehub.
